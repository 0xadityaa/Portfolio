I have shipped side projects that got three upvotes, published posts that felt like shouting into an empty room, and spent late nights refactoring code nobody else will ever read. When you build in public, silence gets to you. No chart going up and to the right, no viral thread, no applause. Part of my brain says: *maybe this just isn't working.*

Then I found a letter Vincent van Gogh wrote in January 1885.

At the time he was painting in obscurity in the village of Nuenen, financially dependent on his brother Theo. He wrote Theo a line I now keep taped to the back of my brain ([letter 480](https://vangoghletters.org/en/let480), Nuenen, on or about Monday, 26 January 1885):

> “…if I’m no good now, I won’t be any good later either — but if later, then now too. For wheat is wheat, even if it looks like grass at first to townsfolk — and the other way round too.”

That image changes how I read quiet seasons of building.

![Short blades above a line of ground, and a much deeper network of roots below it](/images/blog/building-in-silence-roots.svg)

Above ground, early wheat is indistinguishable from grass. Judge by the surface and nothing is happening. Underneath, the roots are doing the work. In a quiet stretch, there are three old ideas I come back to.

## Value and validation are not the same thing

I confuse these two constantly. Market validation is loud and obvious: GitHub stars, revenue, impressions, people sharing your link. Intrinsic value is quieter: whether the thing I built is actually sharp, honest, and well-crafted.

When someone who has never farmed walks past a field of young wheat and calls it weeds, nothing is wrong with the seed. That is a perception bug in the observer. Remembering that helps whenever I publish something I'm proud of and the internet shrugs.

## I only own the brushstrokes

Van Gogh wasn't a Stoic, yet he landed where Epictetus starts his [Enchiridion](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html): draw a hard line between what is up to me and what isn't.

My hours at the keyboard are up to me. The clarity of my thinking, the care I put into an architecture or a paragraph, the discipline to show up again tomorrow: all mine. Whether an algorithm picks it up or strangers applaud it is out of my hands. Every time I hand my mood to people who can't tell wheat from grass yet, I make myself miserable for nothing.

## Do the work because it is what I do

Marcus Aurelius makes this even simpler in [the Meditations (5.6)](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.5.five.html). He pictures a vine that grows grapes and then moves on to the next season without standing around demanding praise for doing its job. A person who does good work, he writes, doesn't yell for an audience to come admire it. They just move on to the next good thing.

I'm not Zen enough to live there all the time, and I still like it when something I build lands with people. But whenever I am writing, coding, or tinkering in silence, I try to remember I'm not failing. I'm in the grass phase.

I'm keeping my roots down. The harvest never creates the wheat; it only reveals what was growing in the dark all along.
